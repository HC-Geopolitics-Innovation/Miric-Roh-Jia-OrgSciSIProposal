## 1. Introduction

Textual data has provided a rich resource for researchers to answer a wide variety of questions that were otherwise difficult to measure. Prior to the advances enabled through rich textual data and text analysis methods (e.g., topic modeling, keyword extraction), empirical research relied heavily on structured tabular data. However, with the emergence of large language models (LLMs), there is a growing inclination to use these new models to replace earlier text analysis methodologies. This is particularly attractive when working with large and complex data such as 10-K financial statements, policy documents, or corporate websites (xxx; xxx) where focused summaries (e.g., abstracts) do not exist.

As existing studies have shown, large language models can be effective, but only when used carefully, transparently, and with validation (Burbano and Carlson, xxx; xxx). These large language models are particularly attractive when applied to long textual documents where traditional text analysis techniques such as topic modeling or embedding construction struggle (where there is a vast amount of noise in the text data relative to the amount of crucial information). However, large language models can be opaque, non-reproducible, and may introduce noise or make decisions that are unobserved and cannot be directly inspected or verified by researchers. In this paper, we argue that the optimal way of using these tools is a blend of both: developing an LLM-enabled data pipeline to go from raw, unstructured long textual documents to specific variables that can be used in subsequent analysis. The goal of this approach is to make the process transparent and verifiable, such that the benefits of large language models can be deployed while maintaining the advantages of established tools and models, along with the transparency and verifiability expected of research publications.

We argue that large language models excel at two types of critical research tasks, and therefore any type of data pipeline should leverage large language models for these two specific tasks while enabling user evaluation and interpretability at other stages. The two areas where large language models are exceptionally effective are:

1. **Synthesizing text** (generating readable, coherent text based on existing documents); and
2. **Generating code** (producing machine-readable instructions that can be systematically executed and tested).

Established text analysis methods—for which we have proven routines, validation standards, and verifiable properties—remain essential and can be augmented by large language models focusing on these two core strengths while avoiding potential pitfalls.

We develop a data pipeline and apply our proposed methodology to a context relevant for organization researchers: studying how changes in government debate around regulating a technology are associated with changes in the innovation trajectories of firms within that industry. We specifically focus on understanding how the policy debate around regulating technology firms unfolded following the Cambridge Analytica scandal. The fundamental challenge in studying this type of question is identifying which firms were likely impacted by this policy, so that we can compare them to a counterfactual of firms that were credibly not impacted. This information is embedded within textual data, but identifying and structuring it requires developing a careful, verifiable, and transparent data pipeline.

### 1.1. Generative AI-Enabled Pipelines for Information Extraction and Synthesis

We develop a framework that provides an example of a data pipeline involving three data processing tasks that leverage LLMs without devolving full control. These tasks yield verifiable, reproducible, and adaptable pipelines. Researchers can tweak these methods to extract different insights or apply them to data of their choice.

#### Overview of Data Pipeline and Processing Tasks

- **Textual Summarization:** Taking large, complex textual documents that contain considerable noise alongside embedded data, partitioning them into chunks, and using LLMs to generate, synthesize, and aggregate textual summaries. We demonstrate this approach on congressional testimony (Albert to confirm – see Appendix B for data sources and processing), summarizing long and convoluted documents into concise summaries that can subsequently be used for text classification, topic modeling, or semantic distance estimation in more reliable ways.
- **Stance Extraction:** Using LLMs to identify the positions of different actors (e.g., Congress members, firms, government agencies) on specific topics, allowing researchers to distill the nuances of arguments and positions that each individual takes on an issue. We apply this to congressional testimony to identify individual stances on key issues within a format that can be utilized in subsequent research.
- **Debate Relevance to Firm Business Areas:** Using the distilled stances of individuals within the debate, we create a synthesis of the policy debate. This synthesis can then be compared to the business descriptions of firms to identify which firms are subject to the debate. In our empirical context, this means framing the key issues that policymakers are debating and attempting to regulate. We then compare this to the 10-K financial statements of firms describing their business operations. This allows us to identify and study firms that are subject to these policies versus those that are not.

### 1.2. Reproducing These AI-Driven Pipelines for Alternative Research Questions

Our methodology thus far leverages the power of large language models for text extraction, synthesis, and data cleaning. However, these models also excel at writing code that is automatically tested, verified, and validated. By providing our pipeline’s code and methodologies in a GitHub repository where the codebase is available, researchers can use their own coding agents to either improve upon our methodology or apply this methodology to their own setting and data.

We believe this provides a framework that can guide how large language models can be used to adapt and deploy research pipelines in the future.

## 2. Why an AI-Enabled Data Pipeline vs. Allowing an LLM to Handle the Entire Task?

Limitations to using long-form textual data are often a hidden constraint in research. First, researchers avoid contexts where they cannot obtain clean, standardized textual data. Second, when using these tools, researchers may introduce considerable noise into the process, as long textual documents may overemphasize noise relative to the signal latent in the data. LLMs seem to be a viable solution to this, and one question that inevitably emerges is why researchers should develop a structured, LLM-enabled pipeline rather than simply prompting a frontier LLM to process entire context windows and execute the entire task end-to-end. Even if current models have limitations, one might worry that future frontier models will continue to advance, overcoming these limitations.

There has been a surge of attempts to use large language models to fundamentally automate and replicate parts of the research process that are laboriously performed by researchers. These include attempts to use LLMs for theorizing (xxx), testing assumptions (xxx), performing classification (xxx), and clustering data (xxx). However, any attempts to use such tools should be met with caution, as their internal processes are fundamentally opaque; devolving control, decision-making, and outputs to such a model should be met with skepticism, or at least require transparency by documenting choices and outputs at each step of the process so that they can be replicated.

We argue that there are three critical reasons for using LLMs as part of a carefully crafted research pipeline rather than fully devolving control:

- **Transparency and Control:** Concerns regarding LLM model transparency, reliability, and stability typically arise when researchers cede full autonomy to a model and ask it to arrive at an unassisted solution. The internal choices, decisions, and intermediate outputs are not visible or verifiable. By using an LLM to execute a specific step (where it is superior to alternatives) within a larger research design (e.g., topic modeling of synthesized summaries), researchers can ensure their designs are reproducible, clear, and verifiable.
- **Auditability and Systematic Replication:** While LLMs can formulate reasoning internally, their outputs often depend heavily on subtle nuances in prompt formulation, model versioning, and temperature settings. By carefully engineering a structured research pipeline, researchers can leverage LLMs at discrete intermediate steps while fully documenting every phase of the process at scale. This modular approach provides more consistent outputs.
- **Model Context Windows:** Even though frontier language models have large context windows (allowing them to process long text documents), they still do so in an opaque way and may miss parts of documents or present them in an inconsistent manner, especially when summarizing corpora of documents. Carefully designing and partitioning this process ensures that data extraction remains reliable, transparent, and reproducible regardless of underlying model advances.

This often-hidden constraint has impacted research in two ways. First, researchers avoid contexts where they cannot obtain clean, standardized textual data. However, as many published papers demonstrate, doing so requires carefully understanding, validating, and verifying how these models perform in a given context, avoiding noise introduced across contexts.

By demonstrating how textual data processes can be broken down into a pipeline of discrete steps, with LLMs deployed only for specific tasks, our methodology provides two sets of contributions:

1. **A pipeline for summarizing and extracting text from large documents**, creating an information-rich synthesis.

### A.1. Long-Document Text Summarization

Many textual documents contain important information but also contain noisy and complex textual data. Text summarization takes such a document and uses a large language model to write a concise, information-rich account of it that can be utilized in subsequent research tasks. In our pipeline, that account is the hearing summary from which Appendix A.2 extracts the topics of debate, so what the summary keeps and what it drops determines what every later step can see.

Off-the-shelf models can summarize an uploaded document in a single call. However, summarizing a large corpus into consistent and representative summaries requires an engineered pipeline, for two reasons. First, long documents still exceed the context limits of economical, open-weight, or local models, which are precisely the models that a researcher can run over thousands of documents at a fixed cost; the researcher must therefore either partition and sequence the text through the model deliberately or accept opaque, automated chunking. Second, long documents move across topics and speakers, so each input must be as coherent as possible for its summary to be faithful; even when a whole document fits in the context window, cleaning and structured partitioning improve the fidelity and clarity of the result. Carefully designing and partitioning this process ensures that data extraction remains reliable, transparent, and reproducible regardless of underlying model advances.

#### A.1.1. Overview of Processing Pipeline

A pipeline to manage this process involves three decisions, which Figure S1 lays out in processing order:

- **Understand Document Structure and Define Semantic Partitioning:** Researchers must first decide what the final summary has to preserve, because the research question determines whether it should capture broad themes, particular arguments, or statements attributed to individual actors. Long textual documents often have a particular structure, and the chunking strategy should follow it. Context-aware chunking uses meaningful units already present in the source, such as a document section or a speaker's uninterrupted contribution, and is the first choice. When such units cannot be identified, recursive structure-aware splitting falls back on paragraphs and then on sentences. When neither is available, a fixed-size sliding window over characters provides a simpler fallback, but it can divide an argument or separate it from its speaker. The units are then grouped into model inputs that fit a chosen context window, leaving room for the instructions and the generated text.
- **Summarize and Combine the Inputs:** Each chunk of text is provided to an LLM with instructions outlining the desired format of the summary and the key information that should be extracted. There are two standard ways to combine the results. In the "map-reduce" approach, each chunk is summarized independently in a map stage, and the segment summaries are combined into one document summary in a reduce stage; when the segment summaries are too long to enter one call, intermediate collapse steps shorten groups of them first. Separate prompts distinguish reading source text from combining existing summaries. This approach runs in parallel at scale, but it can lose context that spans two chunks. In the "refine" approach, the model updates a running summary as successive chunks arrive, which maintains continuity but requires sequential processing and can carry an early error into every later update. Which approach suits a corpus depends on the documents and on the intended output.
- **Model Selection, Validation, and Deployment:** This is inherently an iterative process. Outcomes depend on the choice of model, the prompt, and the aggregation rules, so researchers prototype alternative models and settings on a few documents before scaling. The intermediate outputs make these choices inspectable: the partitions show what each call saw, the segment summaries show what survived the first pass, and the lengths, call counts, and retention of speaker names show the cost and the character of each model's output. Where an external reference exists, such as a human-written abstract, the summaries can be validated against it. Once the choices are fixed, the pipeline is deployed across the entire corpus, and every intermediate artifact is archived: the cleaned source segments, the chunk summaries, the aggregated outputs, and a log of every model call. This record lets a researcher inspect one component or rerun one transformation with a different model without repeating the whole run.

#### A.1.2. Application: Summarizing Long Text Congressional Hearings

We apply this pipeline to a corpus of U.S. congressional hearing transcripts. Hearings record policy deliberations among legislators and invited witnesses, including scientists, corporate executives, and industry stakeholders, and they have served as a source across research settings (xxx; xxx; xxx). However, the transcripts are long and saturated with procedural preamble and formulaic courtesies that dilute the substantive debate. Our sample is the 2,352 hearings of the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology and the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation with usable digitized transcripts on GovInfo, held between 1996 and 2025 and drawn from the 36,378 hearings that GovInfo lists for 1995 to 2025. The median transcript contains 36,505 words. A transcript opens with introductory material and participant lists, followed by opening statements, witness testimony, exchanges between legislators and witnesses, and material inserted into the record.

The research question that the pipeline serves, how congressional debate on regulating a technology relates to firms' innovation in that technology, needs from each hearing the particular practices and proposals discussed and who took a position on them. Appendix A.2 builds the topic codebook and the legislators' stances from these summaries and turns, and Appendix A.3 condenses the stances on consumer data privacy into the policy summary that is compared with firms' business descriptions, which the toy example in Appendix C links to firms' privacy patents. A title or an opening statement names the announced subject, but it cannot supply the positions taken in later exchanges, and procedural recognitions and repeated courtesies occupy space without adding substance. We therefore process the retained hearing record in sequence and ask the model to distinguish substantive discussion from procedural segments.

To show each step concretely, we use *Facebook, Social Media Privacy, and the Use and Abuse of Data* (`CHRG-115shrg37801`) as a running example. This joint hearing of the Senate Judiciary and Commerce committees on April 10, 2018 examined Facebook's handling of user data after the Cambridge Analytica revelations, with testimony from Mark Zuckerberg. Its stored text contains 243,434 words, 385 pages in the printed record, including written material appended to the hearing. The linked [worked example](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/README.md>) provides the source, the parsed records, the exact model inputs, and the recorded outputs; it includes an intermediate collapse stage and a matched C-SPAN abstract, so that both the aggregation and the validation can be followed on one hearing. The same hearing continues as the running example of Appendices A.2 and A.3.

**1. Parse the transcript into speaker records.** We downloaded each transcript and its MODS metadata through GovInfo's API and extracted the fixed-width text from the transcript's HTML wrapper. Because the research question needs legislators' contributions distinguished from witness testimony, together with the exchanges that give them context, the *speaker turn* is the unit we preserve. The downloaded text does not label turns, so we recover them from a recurring formatting pattern: paragraphs begin with at least four spaces, and a turn opens with a title and a surname, such as `Chairman Grassley.` or `Mr. Zuckerberg.` in this hearing. Python reconstructs paragraphs from the indentation, starts a new record at each recognized opening, and matches detected surnames to the participant list in the metadata, attaching Bioguide identifiers to legislators; witnesses keep their transcript label. We remove paragraphs with dot leaders, which mark contents and index entries, drop records flagged as written questions for the record (`qfr`), strip page markers, and standardize whitespace. The [parsed examples](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/T1.4.1 - Example - The Facebook Hearing Step by Step.ipynb>) show these fields and the rules, which require no LLM calls.

For the Facebook hearing, the [source text](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/source.txt>) yields 8,192 paragraphs. Removing 40 dot-leader paragraphs and grouping the remainder produces [903 speaker records](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/parsed_turns.json>), each with the attributed speaker, the member identifier where available, the section label, and the text in transcript order. The output also reveals a limitation: the parser flags no record as `qfr`, although the appendix contains written responses, which therefore remain attached to Grassley's final record and enter summarization.

**2. Group consecutive speaking turns into model inputs.** Preserving turns prevents a contribution from being cut arbitrarily. However, summarizing each turn on its own would detach an answer from the question that explains it, and because the map stage summarizes each input independently, later aggregation cannot restore context that the segment summaries omitted. We therefore pack neighboring turns, in order and intact wherever possible, into inputs of at most 6,144 tokens, measured with the summarization model's tokenizer. This window leaves room for the instructions and the generated text, fits the context limits of the four models we compared (`Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B`, `meta-llama/Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct`, `google/gemma-4-E4B-it`, and `meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct`), and reduces the number of calls relative to one call per turn. When the next turn would exceed the window, a new chunk starts; a turn that exceeds the window on its own is divided at paragraph boundaries and, failing that, at token boundaries, and the [chunking function](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.2 - Process Entire Sample Congressional Hearings/T1.2.1 - Production - Summarize the Sample in Shards.ipynb>) records the resulting lengths and every split.

The running example produces [55 chunks](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/Chunks/chunks.md>). The [second chunk](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/Chunks/chunks_01-10.md#chunk-02>) holds nine records and 5,898 tokens, including Zuckerberg's opening statement and his exchange with Grassley on whether Facebook had required audits to confirm the deletion of improperly transferred data, so that the model sees the question and the reply together. Boundaries are set by length, so a question and its answer can still fall in different chunks, and chunks 8 to 14 divide one long record of submitted letters and statements labeled Grassley. The saved inputs make these exceptions visible; preserving the speaking turn is the rule, and splitting is used only to fit the window.

**3. Summarize each chunk.** Before production, the [model-comparison notebook](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.1 - Process Prototyping and Evaluation/T1.1.2 - Compare - Four Models on Five Hearings.ipynb>) ran the four candidate models on the same five hearings, chosen near the 10th, 30th, 50th, 70th, and 90th percentiles of transcript length, and recorded processing time, call counts, summary lengths, and the retention of speaker names. The comparison profiles feasibility and the character of each model's output; it does not establish accuracy, which we judged by reading a small set of summaries. We selected `Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B` in bfloat16 precision for production. Each chunk then enters one model call. The [map prompt](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/prompts.md#map-prompt>) requests about 150 words on the subjects discussed, the positions attributed to named speakers, and the numbers and legislation copied from the input, which are the fields later steps need to distinguish policies and speakers; for a purely procedural or ceremonial segment it requests one sentence, so that such segments shrink without the model inventing substance. These are instructions to the model, not guarantees that every requested detail survives. In the example, 55 calls produce [55 segment summaries](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/02 - Outputs/outputs.md#map-01>).

**4. Condense the segment summaries when necessary.** The segment summaries must be brought together so that one call can write an account of the whole hearing. For long hearings, however, even these shorter summaries can exceed the 6,144-token window when combined. Python checks their combined length in transcript order; when it is too long, it groups consecutive summaries by token count and asks the model to condense each group with the [collapse prompt](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/prompts.md#collapse-prompt>), which identifies the input as existing summaries, requests about 200 words retaining named speakers, their positions, numbers, and legislation, and instructs the model not to draw a conclusion about the hearing as a whole, since each group covers only part of it. The check repeats on the condensed accounts for up to eight rounds, and hearings whose segment summaries already fit skip the step. In the Facebook example, the 55 summaries are grouped as 1 to 23, 24 to 45, and 46 to 55 into [three condensed accounts](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/02 - Outputs/outputs.md#collapse-01>), which fit together, so one round suffices. Each condensation can omit details; we therefore retain both the segment summaries and the intermediate accounts, so that readers can see what survived.

**5. Produce the hearing-level summary.** In the reduce stage, the model receives the segment summaries, or their condensed versions, in transcript order in a single call. The [reduce prompt](<../T1 - Text Summarization/T1.4 - Running Example [Facebook-Cambridge Analytica Scandal Hearing]/01 - Inputs/prompts.md#reduce-prompt>) requests an account of 200 to 250 words covering the hearing's purpose, main subjects, speakers' positions, disagreements, and key numbers and legislation, and it instructs the model to omit details that the supplied summaries do not establish. The word range is a prompt target; generation is capped at 512 tokens of the summarization model's tokenizer, which keeps the output short for downstream text analysis and within the input limits of BERT-based models such as BERTopic, and the pipeline removes an incomplete final sentence when the cap is reached. **7. Validate the summaries against independent human-written abstracts.** A fluent summary can omit central points or add claims that the transcript does not support, so we assess whether the summaries capture the hearings' substantive content. Overlap measures such as ROUGE compare words and sequences with a human reference and do not by themselves establish whether meaning is preserved; we therefore use independent human-written accounts as references and assess correspondence in meaning, allowing for differences in wording. C-SPAN's editorial abstracts provide such a reference for the hearings it broadcast. However, they are much shorter than our summaries and cover only the subject and selected points, so a detail absent from the abstract may still be supported by the transcript, and a similarity score on its own cannot show whether the resemblance reflects the particular hearing or the vocabulary common to many policy discussions. We therefore ask whether a generated summary resembles the abstract of its own hearing more closely than the abstracts of other hearings, including hearings of the same committee.

We matched hearings to C-SPAN programs by date and chamber, title similarity, and witness names, without using the generated summaries; of the 2,352 hearings, 351 had a matched abstract. ### A.2. Extracting Topics, Positions, and Arguments from Long-Form Documents

Large language models can also extract subtle but important nuances from textual documents, specifically related to topics of interest. In this step, we provide a method for extracting topics, positions, and arguments from long-form documents. The method runs in two tracks. Track 1 turns each document's summary from Appendix A.1 into a small set of named subjects and builds the codebook of topics that all later work uses. Track 2 returns to the raw text and attaches to those topics the specific statements that individual actors made about them, with their direction and the sentences that carry them. Throughout, a *subject* is a phrase extracted from a document's summary, an *issue* is what a statement in the raw text is about, and a *topic* is a codebook category into which both are placed. The statements on consumer data privacy that Track 2 produces are the input to the policy summary of Appendix A.3.

The standardized summaries could be supplied directly to established topic models such as LDA. However, extraction with an LLM differs in four ways that matter for a research question about regulation. First, the extraction is instructed: the model is told what counts as a subject for the research question and what does not, such as an agency named in passing, and it returns the central subjects of each document in order of centrality, whereas a topic model weighs an incidental mention the same as the central subject. Second, the finest level of detail is the named subject itself, so fine distinctions within one policy domain survive, where a topic model confined to one domain yields finer topics dominated by shared vocabulary. Third, meaning enters at two points rather than only when the researcher reads the output: the model returns a readable phrase, and the phrases are grouped by embedding their meaning, so that "Facebook data privacy" and "GDPR-style controls" can enter one category although they share no word. Fourth, a label alone cannot show how a topic is discussed; Track 2 returns to the raw text and attaches the specific statements made about a topic, so that with the topic held fixed the statements capture how its treatment varies over time and across speakers.

#### A.2.1. Overview of Processing Pipeline

A pipeline to manage this process involves five steps, which Figure S2 lays out in two tracks:

- **Extract Topics from Textual Summaries:** From the long-form textual summaries, researchers can identify the major topics discussed within each document. Because the summaries are condensed and uniform, one model call per document is enough. The rule for what counts as a subject is written *prior to* extraction and applied through the prompt: it states what a subject is, such as a specific policy, program, regulation, or practice, and what does not qualify, such as an actor named by itself or an item mentioned only as an example. The model returns a short ranked list of phrases that can be checked against the summary. Different documents name related subjects in different wording, so the phrases are embedded and clustered on meaning into candidate groups at several levels of detail. An LLM then drafts a label, a definition, and example phrases for each candidate group, and researchers review every group and record a decision to keep, merge, or drop it. The result is frozen as the codebook, and each document is classified by the topic that holds most of its subjects.
- **Filter Textual Summaries by Topic Relevance:** The raw text contains the subtle and nuanced arguments that a summary compresses, attributes to no one, or drops. However, most of it is noise for a question about positions: generic statements, procedure, courtesy, factual description, and remarks by peripheral actors. The raw text is therefore prepared as passages that preserve attribution, such as a speaker's turn, with their sentences numbered, and the model's first judgment in each passage is a selection: which sentences, if any, express a position that qualifies under the rule. All other sentences return nothing, and the prompt closes with "when in doubt, do not extract," because an invented position enters the counts while a missed one only lowers them. The selection does not see the document's Track 1 subjects, so that a broad subject cannot make every sentence count as relevant.
- **Extract Arguments and Stances from Relevant Textual Summaries:** Within the selected sentences, the model names the *issue* as a short neutral phrase that carries no direction, and labels the *stance* toward it from a closed set, here support or concern. Each record cites the one to three consecutive sentence numbers that carry it, so that any record can be traced to its source and reviewed. Each judgment is an inference, but a narrow one: the model selects sentences in front of it, names an issue those sentences contain, and chooses a label from a closed set. Rule-based code then screens every record against the passage it came from, rejects malformed output and records whose cited sentences do not exist or are not consecutive, flags issue phrases that carry a direction, and merges records that repeat one stance within a passage.
- **Aggregate and Summarize:** The statements took no part in the clustering, so they are placed under the fixed topics by the similarity of their issue to each topic's member phrases, with a threshold below which they remain unassigned; whether a document's statements fall in the topics Track 1 assigned to the document is a cross-check on both tracks. Topics can then be grouped by hand into broader groups suited to the research question. Aggregation requires three choices: the counting unit (a statement measures the volume of discussion; a speaker measures how many hold a view), the comparison period (here the two-year Congress), and the balance measure that summarizes direction within a topic and period, reported together with the underlying counts. We use the share of supporting statements among all statements with a direction, with uncertainty clustered by document.
- **Prototyping and Validation:** The goal of this exercise is to extract information that is otherwise difficult to capture and contextualize at scale. Prompts and parsers are fixed only after development on small seeded samples read turn by turn beside the extracted records. Format checks establish that records are well formed, not that they are correct, so samples of records are read against their cited sentences. Comprehensive ground truth does not pre-exist; where an independent classification of the documents exists, the topics are benchmarked against it, with frequency and random baselines so that agreement due to the subject distribution can be separated from agreement due to the document's own content.

#### A.2.2. Application: Extracting Stances and Arguments around Focal Topics

We apply this methodology to extract stances and arguments from U.S. congressional hearings. The research question needs two kinds of information from each hearing: the regulatory subjects it focused on, which later connect hearings to firms, and the stances legislators took on particular issues within those subjects, which measure regulatory concern and its direction. Track 1 extracts hearing-level subjects from the 2,352 summaries of Appendix A.1 and builds the codebook; Track 2 returns to the transcripts and places legislators' statements into that codebook; a final step groups the topics by hand and counts the statements by group and Congress. We continue with the Facebook hearing of April 10, 2018 as the running example. The linked [topic extraction example](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Worked Example/README.md>) contains the numbered inputs, the exact prompts, the recorded responses, the category assignments, and the verification code.

**Track 1. Hearing-level subjects**

**1. Select the source text.** Track 1 takes each hearing's final summary from Appendix A.1 as its input, because the summaries present each hearing's main subjects in a short and uniform form and one call per hearing is enough to identify them.

**2. Design the extraction and develop the prompt.** The LLM is given one hearing's summary together with the [Track 1 prompt](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Background Helper Functions/stance.py>) and decides which subjects the summary gives substantial attention to, naming each as a short neutral phrase. Without a rule, the first development rounds returned names such as an agency or a bill number, which say who or which bill was discussed but not what the hearing was about and cannot be compared across hearings. The prompt therefore sets a screening rule: a subject is a specific policy or program, a regulation or technology, or a practice or budget item; an agency, company, or country named by itself does not count, and neither does a bare bill number or an entity mentioned only as an example or comparison. The [Track 1 parser](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Background Helper Functions/stance.py>) checks that the output is a list of strings, drops case-insensitive duplicates and any phrase beyond the fifth, flags directional wording, and counts each dropped item. Two judgments are left to the model, whether a phrase is central and whether it names the right subject, and both are low-risk because the input is already a standardized summary. The prompt was fixed after two development rounds on 28 seeded summaries; the recorded version is `summary-topics-0.2-dev`.

**3. Extract hearing-level subjects.** We use `Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B` under the same settings as Appendix A.1 and send each summary to the model once with the Track 1 prompt ([production notebook](<../B - Backup of Original File Structure/scripts/python/03_topic_extraction/04_track1_production.ipynb>)). For the Facebook hearing, the [saved response](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Worked Example/02 - Outputs/topics.json>) is the following.

```json
["Facebook data privacy", "content moderation", "election integrity", "Honest Ads Act", "GDPR-style controls"]
```

The prompt asks for up to five phrases ordered from most to least central, and the parser keeps them with their ranks. Across all 2,352 hearings, the extraction yields 11,592 mentions and 9,095 distinct subjects after standardizing capitalization and whitespace; hearings received 4.93 phrases on average with a standard deviation of 0.28.

**4. Cluster the subjects into candidate groups.** Hearings on the same matter rarely yield the same wording, so comparing hearings requires that subjects with similar meanings be grouped before any category is defined. The [clustering notebook](<../B - Backup of Original File Structure/scripts/python/03_topic_extraction/08_taxonomy_consensus.ipynb>) first strips years, bill numbers, and trailing words such as "Act" from each phrase, so that "California Consumer Privacy Act" and "California Consumer Privacy" become one string, embeds the 8,736 resulting strings with `BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5`, and groups near-duplicates into 7,712 families. It then partitions the families at nine candidate counts from 15 to 150, repeating each partition 30 times on subsamples to see whether the groupings persist when the fitting sample changes. We examine three properties of each partition: stability (how consistently members are assigned together across repetitions), silhouette (how well clusters are separated from their neighbors), and cohesion (how closely member meanings align within a cluster). None is used as a gate. The 15-cluster partition has the highest stability and silhouette of all counts, and among the finer partitions the 150-cluster partition has the highest stability and the fewest low-cohesion groups, so we keep 15 broad clusters and 150 candidate topics; the diagnostics are saved with the clustering notebook. These are chosen levels of detail and not optimal counts.

**5. Review the codebook and classify each hearing.** We review the 150 candidate groups before any assignment, because a plausible label alone cannot establish a coherent group. The [drafting notebook](<../B - Backup of Original File Structure/scripts/python/03_topic_extraction/09_codebook_draft.ipynb>) shows the model each group's member subjects and the leading subjects of its two nearest groups; the model drafts a label, a one-sentence definition, a note on how the group differs from its neighbors, and five example phrases copied from the members (the prompt is in that notebook), and a parser removes any invented example. We then review every draft. The review keeps 111 groups, merges nine into kept groups, and drops 30. Dropped groups are held together by a shared word rather than by meaning, such as "program" or "initiative", or mix unrelated subjects; this is a limit of clustering on the wording of a bare name alone. Merged groups join a kept group on the same matter, as the Clean Air and Clean Water Act subjects join EPA regulation. The [codebook of 111 topics](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Worked Example/02 - Outputs/codebook.json>) is frozen with a version hash, so that every later assignment refers to the same topics. Each kept topic also sits under the broad cluster holding most of its subjects, which leaves 14 broad themes; these serve only the validation of step 6, because the analysis groups the topics by hand in step 11. The frozen entry for the topic at the center of the running example reads as follows.

| Field | Entry for topic 68 |
|---|---|
| Label | consumer data privacy legislation |
| Definition | This topic encompasses federal and state laws, proposed bills, and regulatory frameworks specifically addressing the collection, use, and protection of consumer personal information. |

**6. Validate against an independent classification.** The Comparative Agendas Project (CAP) codes congressional hearings into human-assigned *major topics* and *subtopics*. Our codebook uses its own categories, so we examine correspondence between the two systems rather than requiring identical labels. The [matching notebook](<../B - Backup of Original File Structure/scripts/python/03_topic_extraction/07_cap_matching.ipynb>) links hearings to CAP rows by Congress and committee and then by date and title, without reading CAP's topic codes. That subtopic ranks first for 27.0 percent of hearings and within the top 11 for 63.1 percent, with a median rank of fifth, where a random ranking would place it in the top 5 percent of 213 candidates for 1 hearing in 20. (b) Because these committees concentrate on particular subjects, we compare the phrase-based ranking with a frequency rule that gives every hearing the same list of the most common subtopics. With one candidate the phrase finds the human code for 27.0 percent of hearings and the frequency rule for 9.4 percent; with 11 candidates the shares are 63.1 and 52.9 percent, so the correspondence comes from what each summary says about its own hearing rather than from knowing which subjects these committees usually take up. **Track 2. Legislators' stances within topics**

Regulatory concern is expressed in what legislators say about a topic, which a summary may compress, attribute to no one, or drop. Track 2 therefore returns to the transcripts and adds to each hearing-level topic the stances legislators expressed about it. It reuses the model, the sentence numbering, and the screening discipline of Track 1, but during extraction it does not reference Track 1, for the reason given in step 8; the placement in step 10 then serves as the cross-check between the tracks.

**7. Prepare the speaker turns.** Track 2 takes each legislator's own speaker turn as its input; witness turns are excluded because the measure is of legislators' stances, which can reach firms through regulation. The turns come from the parsing of Appendix A.1. The [attribution rules](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Background Helper Functions/speakers.py>) read the honorific and surname opening a paragraph, attach a Bioguide identifier when the surname appears on the committee roster, and carry the presiding chair, written as "The Chairman" without a surname, under the placeholder identifier `0000000`. The [preparation notebook](<../B - Backup of Original File Structure/scripts/python/03_topic_extraction/01_member_turns.ipynb>) selects turns labeled as spoken and attributed to a committee member or to the chair: 221,311 of the 399,029 spoken turns (55 percent), from 2,343 of the 2,352 hearings. We split each turn into sentences with `pysbd==0.3.4` and apply four cleaning rules implemented in the [stance helper](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Background Helper Functions/stance.py>): truncate the turn at the first adjournment or inserted-document marker, drop the leading speaker-label echo ("Senator Dorgan."), drop bracketed stage directions, and drop mostly capitalized inline headers. Of 2,490,103 raw sentences, truncation removes 1,249,776 (50.2 percent), label echoes 219,650, headers 28,666, and stage directions 6,350, leaving 985,661 sentences in 220,160 nonempty turns. From the Facebook hearing's 903 speaker records, this step selects 507 legislator turns containing 1,696 cleaned sentences; truncation removed 13,380 sentences, almost all from Grassley's final record with its appended written responses. Sentence numbering starts at one within each cleaned turn, so that the model can point to existing evidence without generating a quotation, and turns longer than 40 sentences are sent in consecutive windows that keep the turn-wide numbering.

**8. Design the extraction and develop the prompt.** The LLM is given one speaker turn with its numbered sentences and the speaker's name, together with the [Track 2 prompt](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Background Helper Functions/stance.py>), and nothing else: not the witness's answer, not the neighboring turns, not the hearing's subject or the codebook. If Track 2 selected sentences by Track 1's subject, most sentences in the hearing would count as relevant and the extraction would lose its discrimination. Within the turn the model makes three judgments in order. The first is the *selection* of sentences that express a stance toward a policy issue; questions asked without a stance, procedure, courtesies, introductions, and factual statements return nothing, and the prompt closes with "when in doubt, do not extract." The second is the *issue*, named as a short neutral noun phrase that never carries the direction, so that support for an issue and concern about the same issue can enter one category later. The third is the *direction*: `support` if the legislator endorses or urges adoption, funding, or expansion of the issue, and `concern` if the legislator expresses worry or objection toward it or urges its restriction, delay, or scrutiny; a harm is recorded as concern and never as support. Each record holds one to three consecutive sentence numbers, the issue, and the direction.

The [Track 2 parser](<../T2 - Topic Extraction and Stance Analysis/T2.X - Background Helper Functions/stance.py>) rejects output that is not well-formed JSON or uses another stance label, and any record whose cited sentence numbers do not exist in the turn, exceed three, or are not consecutive; every rejection is recorded with its reason. In the full run this rule discarded 4,680 records against 32,385 kept, so a stance the model grounded in four sentences is thrown away rather than trimmed. The parser also checks the issue against a fixed dictionary of sixteen directional word stems, such as "ban" or "expand", and flags matching records without removing them; 838 of the kept records (2.59 percent) carry the flag, 326 of them from the word "protection" in neutral names such as consumer protection, and the review of the remaining flags is still to be done. It then merges records that share an issue and a direction when their cited sentences overlap or lie within two sentences of each other, so that a stance stated three times in one turn counts once. The [stance promptbook](<../B - Backup of Original File Structure/scripts/python/03_topic_extraction/02_extraction_promptbook.ipynb>) iterated the prompt on 30 seeded turns of varied length from both chambers, including short procedural turns whose correct output is an empty list, and every round was read turn by turn beside the extracted records; three rounds removed the observed failure classes, which included appended written material, truncated opening statements, and a harm recorded as the supported issue. The recorded version is `stance-extraction-0.3-dev`, and the wording did not change after the full run began.

### A.3. Policy Relevance for Publicly Listed Firms

The earlier analysis provides detailed information extraction from congressional debate about economically important policies that directly shape the external environment of publicly listed firms. While there are anecdotal examples of firms being influenced by these policies, it is difficult to quantify this at scale and determine the extent to which firms are impacted. The statements of Appendix A.2 say what legislators want regulated and in which direction; what is missing is a measure, for every firm, of how close its business is to what the debate is about.

To quantitatively measure the relevance of policy debate to publicly listed firms requires computing the relatedness between policy documents and corporate disclosures that reflect the business of the firm. This is fundamentally achieved by computing the textual distance between a document conveying the policy debate and a document describing the firm's business. However, neither side is a single short document: the debate on one topic is spread over hundreds of statements in dozens of hearings, and a firm's business is described in a 10-K Item 1 section that runs to tens of thousands of words and changes from year to year. We therefore use the LLM on both sides first, as a summarizer, to produce two short texts that a researcher can read in a minute and that an embedding model can represent whole: a "policy summary" of what the legislation discussed in the statements asks of companies and tries to regulate, and a "firm-level summary" of each firm's lines of business across its filings. The cosine similarity between the two is the firm's topic relevance. This is the measure used throughout this proposal; alternative constructions of the score, which replace one side or the other with sentence-level comparisons or with other representations of the topic, are reported separately and are not described here.

#### A.3.1. Overview of Processing Pipeline

A pipeline to manage this process involves three steps, which Figure S4 lays out as two sides that meet in one computation:

- **Identify & Pre-Process Focal Documents:** On the topic side, the focal documents are the statements that Appendix A.2 placed under the focal topic, each carrying the sentences a legislator spoke and the legislator's direction. They are summarized in two stages with the map-reduce pattern of Appendix A.1: one call per hearing year writes what the legislation discussed that year asks of companies and tries to regulate, and one further call combines the yearly summaries into the policy summary. On the firm side, the focal documents are the Item 1 (Business) sections of the firm's 10-K filings, one per fiscal year. Each Item 1 is cut into segments and summarized with a prompt that keeps only the firm's lines of business, the products, services, or segments it sells or operates and who its customers are, and excludes internal activities such as labor relations, financing, or regulatory compliance; one further call per firm condenses its yearly summaries into the firm-level summary, noting changes across the years. Both summaries are kept with their inputs and call records, so that any claim in them can be traced to the statements or the filing text it came from.
- **Compute Semantic Distance and Topical Relevance:** Both summaries are embedded with the same sentence encoder, `BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5`, as unit-length vectors, so that the cosine similarity of the two is their dot product. The score is one number per firm. A higher score means that the firm's business description is closer to what the legislation regulates; it measures semantic relatedness, not the firm's stance toward the legislation and not its compliance. Summaries rather than raw texts enter the comparison for three reasons: a 10-K Item 1 exceeds the encoder's input limit many times over, its boilerplate would dominate a raw comparison, and the summaries make the two texts that are compared readable, so that a reader can judge for any firm why its score is high or low.
- **Aggregate Distance and Relevance Measures:** The firm-level summary already pools a firm's filings, so its cosine with the policy summary is the firm's score over the sample period. The yearly summaries give, in addition, one cosine per filing, which can be read as a firm-year series or averaged. Scores are reported relative to the sample mean or median, by NAICS sector and subsector, and as quartiles across firms, which is how the toy example of Appendix C uses them. The main sample is every fiscal year a firm filed (the all-years sample); a second sample of three fiscal years (2010, 2015, and 2020) is kept for comparison.

#### A.3.2. Application: Connecting Focal Concerns and Opportunities of Policymakers to Business Areas of Publicly Listed Firms

We apply this methodology to understand how congressional policy debate around a particular policy topic relates to the business of firms. We focus on a particularly transformational shift in regulatory debate: the regulation of technology companies in relation to consumer privacy following the Cambridge Analytica scandal, which affected Facebook. The focal topic is therefore consumer data privacy legislation (topic 68 of the codebook), the topic whose support share rose from the 115th Congress onward in Figure 2, and the firms are the S&P 500 constituents whose 10-K filings describe their businesses over the same period.

The firm texts come from the SEC EDGAR 10-K archive, collected in the data stage of the project: the bulk annual-report containers were downloaded through `sec-api.io`, filings were linked to Compustat firm identifiers through the CIK–GVKEY crosswalk, and the Item 1 (Business) section was extracted for every filing of a firm that was ever in the S&P 500 (Appendix B gives the sources and their counts). The resulting table holds 22,299 filings of 1,061 firms. The [sample notebook](<../T3 - Topic Relevance and Corporate Disclosure/TEMP/T3.4 - Sample/T3.4.1 - Sample - Select Firms and Filings.ipynb>) drops 333 filings whose Item 1 could not be extracted and 3,164 outside fiscal years 1999 to 2025, and resolves 23 firm-years that carry two usable filings by keeping the earlier-filed one, which is the ordinary full-year report. This leaves the all-years sample of 18,779 filings of 1,058 firms, between 625 and 738 filings in every fiscal year, and one filing per firm and year. The three-year sample (fiscal 2010, 2015, and 2020) holds 2,072 filings of 841 firms. Meta Platforms, the firm at the center of the running example, enters with 14 filings, fiscal 2012 to 2025.

**Topic side. From statements to the policy summary**

**1. Collect the statements under the focal topic.** The [statement notebook](<../T3 - Topic Relevance and Corporate Disclosure/TEMP/T3.5 - Topic Representations/T3.5.2 - Topic - Rebuild the Stance Statements.ipynb>) collects the 374 statements that Appendix A.2 placed under consumer data privacy legislation: each is the one to three sentences a legislator spoke, with the hearing, the speaker, the hearing year, and the stance as support or concern. They come from 119 hearings held between 1999 and 2025, and the 13 statements of the Facebook hearing listed in Appendix A.2 are among them. No selection is made beyond the placement of Appendix A.2, so the policy summary summarizes what legislators said whenever they took a position on the topic, in either direction.

**2. Summarize the statements by hearing year (map).** The [input notebook](<../T3 - Topic Relevance and Corporate Disclosure/TEMP/T3.5 - Topic Representations/T3.5.4 - Topic - Prepare the Policy Summary Inputs.ipynb>) groups the statements by hearing year, which gives 26 groups with between a handful and several dozen statements each, and the [summary script](<../T3 - Topic Relevance and Corporate Disclosure/TEMP/T3.5 - Topic Representations/T3.5.5 - Topic - Summarize the Statements.py>) sends each group to `Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B` in one call. The system prompt casts the model as a policy analyst who reports only what the statements say. The map prompt presents the year's numbered statements, each marked with the legislator's stance, and asks for at most 150 words of continuous prose on "what the legislation discussed in these statements asks of companies and other organizations and what it tries to regulate: which data practices, which kinds of data, and which actors," without listing or counting the statements, naming legislators, or adding knowledge beyond them. Generation uses temperature 0.1, a fixed seed, and no thinking mode, with a cap of 512 tokens that no call reached. The 26 yearly summaries are kept.

**3. Combine the yearly summaries into the policy summary (reduce).** One further call presents the 26 yearly summaries, each marked with its year, and asks for one summary of 200 to 250 words for a reader who has not seen the statements, covering what the legislation asks of companies and the rights it gives consumers, and what it tries to regulate, naming the specific requirements, data practices, and actors rather than saying that the topic is about privacy in general. ### Evaluating the Impact of Privacy Policy Debate Following the Cambridge Analytica Scandal on the Trajectory of Firm Corporate Research on Privacy Technology

As described above, we demonstrate how the application of our data pipeline to congressional testimony and company 10-K filings allows us to quantify which firms were impacted by this policy and study this question in a way not previously possible. We focus on a specific empirical research question: how the increasing intensity of congressional attention to consumer privacy after 2016 (the Cambridge Analytica scandal) and calls for regulation and legislation were associated with the innovation trajectories of affected companies. Appendix A.2 documents the attention: the share of supporting statements on consumer data privacy legislation rose from the 115th Congress onward (Figure 2). Appendix A.3 identifies the firms: the topic relevance score ranks every sample firm by how close its business description is to what that legislation regulates. What remains is an outcome that records a firm's innovative activity in the technologies the legislation touches, year by year, for the same firms. This appendix describes that outcome, the privacy patents of the sample firms, and the setting in which the relevance scores will be matched to them. The comparison of patenting across firms of high and low relevance, and any design that reads the association causally, follow in a later step and are not reported here.

**The patent data and the link to the sample firms.** We use the extended patent data of Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Stoffman (2017), in the version released in September 2026 that runs to the end of 2025. It is a patent-level panel of granted U.S. patents from 1926 to 2025, each with its filing date, its issue date, the CRSP permno of the assignee at issue, and the authors' estimate of the patent's value, together with a match file that lists every Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) code of every patent. The [linking notebook](<../T4 - Toy Example [Topic Relevance on Privacy Innovation]/T4.1 - Privacy Patents and Topic Relevance/T4.1.1 - Patents - Link Sample Firms to KPSS Patents.ipynb>) attaches these patents to the 1,058 firms of the all-years sample of Appendix A.3 in two steps. First, each firm's Compustat identifier is linked to its CRSP permno or permnos through the CRSP-Compustat link table, keeping the researched links (types LC and LU, primary flags P and C) with their start and end dates: 1,432 links to 1,196 permnos, with 927 firms linked to one permno and 131 to two or more over time. Second, a patent is attributed to the firm whose link is in force on the patent's filing date, so that a patent filed by a subsidiary or a predecessor counts for the sample firm only while the link held. This gives 1,315,652 patents filed between 1999 and 2025 by 719 of the 1,058 firms, 56 percent of all patents filed in the period in the data; 339 firms, among them many banks, insurers, utilities, and retailers, have no patent at all. Because the data cover granted patents and applications filed late in the period were not all granted by 2025, the descriptive figures stop at filing year 2021: 1,238,690 patents by 709 firms.

**The privacy codes.** A privacy patent is a patent of a sample firm that carries at least one of five CPC codes selected by hand for this example, as an exact match of the full code, so that a listed parent code does not pull in unlisted child codes. The five codes, with the plain-language names used in the figures and the definitions in the CPC scheme, are the following.

| Code | Plain-language name | CPC definition |
|---|---|---|
| G06Q 30/0269 | Advertising Targeted by User Profile | based on user profile or attribute |
| G06Q 30/0255 | Advertising Targeted by User History | based on user history |
| G06F 21/6254 | Anonymisation of Personal Data | by anonymising data, e.g. decorrelating personal data from the owner's identification |
| H04L 67/535 | Tracking of User Activity in Network Services | Tracking the activity of the user (network monitoring arrangements H04L 43/00; recording of computer activity G06F 11/34) |
| G06F 11/3438 | Monitoring of User Actions on Computers | monitoring of user actions (tracking the activity of the user H04L 67/535) |

The first two codes are subgroups of targeted advertising, the practice at the center of the Cambridge Analytica hearings; the third is the technique that the policy summary of Appendix A.3 describes as a protection; the last two are the tracking and monitoring of users that legislators asked to restrict. The [descriptive notebook](<../T4 - Toy Example [Topic Relevance on Privacy Innovation]/T4.2 - Privacy Patents and Topic Relevance - 5 Codes by MM/T4.2.2 - Descriptive - Privacy Patents of the Sample Firms, 1999 to 2021.ipynb>) attaches the CPC codes to every sample-firm patent (8,446,709 patent-code pairs) and flags the privacy patents: 3,707 patents, 0.30 percent of the firms' patents filed in 1999 to 2021, by 136 of the 1,058 firms; 922 firms filed none. Figures S6 and S7 show what the outcome contains. The codes therefore separate two kinds of privacy technology, the use of personal data for advertising and the protection and monitoring of it, whose time paths differ, and a design that pools them would mix the two.

## Appendix D. Reproducible and Adaptable Data Pipelines with Coding Agents

While this data pipeline leverages AI tools to perform important analysis tasks, an additional important benefit is emerging in empirical research: research papers made available through platforms such as GitHub in easy machine-readable formats (Markdown, Python code, Stata, and R) can be deployed rapidly through coding agents to build entire data pipelines. In addition to providing a methodology that can advance research with text data, we also advocate for and provide a framework for using online repositories of papers as a medium where researchers can share tools that are easily deployable and adaptable for other scholars. To demonstrate this in Appendix XX, we provide a sample agent prompt and a sample implementation plan generated by an LLM. Researchers can do the same with their own AI tools, augment the implementation plan, and deploy it further.
