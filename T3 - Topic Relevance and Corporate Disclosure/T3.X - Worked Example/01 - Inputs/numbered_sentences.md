[28] Sleep is a complex neurological process that includes two distinct states: rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep and non-rapid eye movement, or non-REM, sleep.
[29] REM sleep, which is about 20-25% of total sleep experienced by adults, is characterized by a high level of brain activity, bursts of rapid eye movement, increased heart and respiration rates, and paralysis of many muscles.
[30] Non-REM sleep is subdivided into four stages that generally parallel sleep depth; stage 1 is the lightest and stage 4 is the deepest.
[31] The upper airway has no rigid support and is held open by active contraction of upper airway muscles.
[32] Normally, during REM sleep and deeper levels of non-REM sleep, upper airway muscles relax and the airway narrows.
[33] Individuals with narrow upper airways or poor muscle tone are prone to temporary collapses of the upper airway during sleep, called apneas, and to near closures of the upper airway called hypopneas.
[34] These breathing events result in a lowering of blood oxygen concentration, causing the central nervous system to react to the lack of oxygen or increased carbon dioxide and signaling the body to respond.
[35] Typically, the individual subconsciously arouses from sleep, causing the throat muscles to contract, opening the airway.
[36] After a few gasping breaths, blood oxygen levels increase and the individual can resume a deeper sleep until the cycle repeats itself.
[37] Sufferers of OSA typically experience ten or more such cycles per hour.
[38] While these awakenings greatly impair the quality of sleep, the individual is not normally aware of these disruptions.
[39] In addition, OSA has been recognized as a cause of hypertension and a significant co-morbidity for heart disease, stroke and diabetes.
